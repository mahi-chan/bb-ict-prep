    Circuit Analysis

    The exam questions here are numerical — Ohm's law, KCL/KVL mesh analysis, Thevenin equivalent, AC power factor, Zener regulator design, clipper and clamper output waveforms, and BJT region identification. Every topic below walks the math on a concrete circuit.

    8 topics

    Circuit Analysis for this exam sits at the intersection of physics and electronics. DC fundamentals (Ohm's, Kirchhoff's, Thevenin) and semiconductor devices (diodes, transistors, op-amps) dominate the questions. AC analysis appears as power-factor and resonance calculations.

    1. DC fundamentals

    Ohm's law is the starting point: V = IR. Current flows from higher to lower potential, and the voltage drop across a resistor equals the product of current through it and its resistance. For a concrete circuit:

    Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω
    I = V / Rtotal = 12 / 6 = 2 A
    VR1 = 2 × 2 = 4 V, VR2 = 2 × 4 = 8 V
    Check: 4 + 8 = 12 V ✓ (KVL)

    Series resistors add: Req = R₁ + R₂ + … . Parallel resistors combine as: 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … . For two in parallel: Req = R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂).

    Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL): the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving. This is conservation of charge.

    Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL): the sum of all voltages around a closed loop is zero. This is conservation of energy.

    Voltage divider: Vout = Vin × R₂/(R₁+R₂). Current divider: for two parallel branches, I₁ = Itotal × R₂/(R₁+R₂).

    Delta-wye (star-delta) transformation converts three resistors in a triangle (delta) to three in a Y (star) or vice versa:

    RY = (adjacent pair product) / (sum of delta)
    RΔ = (sum of Y pair products) / (opposite Y resistor)
    KVL and KCL are all you need to solve any resistive circuit — the network theorems (Thevenin, Norton, superposition) are shortcuts that reduce the work for specific question types.

    2. Network theorems

    Thevenin's theorem: any linear two-terminal network can be replaced by a voltage source Vth in series with a resistance Rth. To find them:

    1. Remove the load and find Voc across the terminals

      This is Vth — the open-circuit voltage.

    2. Deactivate all independent sources and find R looking into the terminals

      Short voltage sources, open current sources. The resulting resistance is Rth.

    3. Reconnect the load to the Thevenin equivalent

      Iload = Vth / (Rth + Rload).

    Norton's theorem: same idea but with a current source IN = Vth/Rth in parallel with RN = Rth. Thevenin and Norton are source transformations of each other.

    Superposition: in a linear circuit with multiple sources, the response (voltage or current) is the sum of responses due to each source acting alone, with all other independent sources deactivated.

    Maximum power transfer: a load receives maximum power when Rload = Rth. The maximum power is Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth). The efficiency at this point is only 50%.

    Pmax = Vth² / (4 Rth)
    Thevenin is the exam's most-asked theorem because the method is mechanical and the answer is numerical. The typical question gives a circuit, asks for Vth and Rth, then asks for load current or maximum power.

    3. Energy storage elements

    Capacitors store energy in an electric field. C = Q/V (farads). Series: 1/Ceq = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂. Parallel: Ceq = C₁ + C₂. Energy: E = ½CV².

    Inductors store energy in a magnetic field. V = L(di/dt). Series: Leq = L₁ + L₂. Parallel: 1/Leq = 1/L₁ + 1/L₂. Energy: E = ½LI².

    RC transients: when a capacitor charges through a resistor from voltage Vs:

    v(t) = Vs(1 − e−t/τ)   [charging]
    v(t) = V₀ e−t/τ   [discharging]
    τ = RC is the time constant; after 5τ the transient is essentially complete

    At t = τ, charging reaches 63.2% of Vs; discharging drops to 36.8% of V₀. These percentages come from 1 − e−1 ≈ 0.632 and e−1 ≈ 0.368.

    Capacitors combine like parallel resistors (series adds reciprocals), inductors combine like series resistors (series adds directly). This is the opposite of resistors — a common exam trap.

    4. AC analysis

    In AC circuits, voltage and current are sinusoidal. The key relationships:

    Vrms = Vpeak / √2 ≈ 0.707 Vpeak
    Vavg = 2Vpeak / π ≈ 0.637 Vpeak (full-wave rectified)

    Impedance extends resistance to AC: ZR = R, ZL = jωL, ZC = 1/(jωC). For a series RLC: Z = R + j(ωL − 1/ωC).

    Resonance occurs when the imaginary part of impedance is zero — the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel:

    ωL = 1/(ωC) → f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

    At resonance, impedance is purely resistive (Z = R), current is maximum (series) or minimum (parallel), and the circuit can amplify voltage across L or C by the Q-factor:

    Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) = f₀/BW
    Higher Q = sharper resonance peak = more selective

    Power in AC circuits:

    PowerFormulaUnitWhat it represents
    Real (P)VI cos φWActual power consumed
    Reactive (Q)VI sin φVARPower stored/returned by L and C
    Apparent (S)VIVATotal power delivered
    Power factor = cos φ = P/S = R/|Z|
    φ is the phase angle between voltage and current

    Power factor correction: most industrial loads are inductive (motors), giving a lagging power factor. Adding a parallel capacitor reduces the reactive power, bringing cos φ closer to 1 and reducing the current drawn from the supply for the same real power.

    The power triangle relates P, Q and S: S² = P² + Q². The angle φ is the same as the impedance angle. A purely resistive load has pf = 1; a purely reactive load has pf = 0.

    5. Diodes and rectifiers

    A PN junction diode conducts in forward bias (anode positive) with a turn-on voltage of ~0.7 V (Si) or ~0.3 V (Ge), and blocks in reverse bias until the breakdown voltage.

    Rectifiers convert AC to pulsating DC:

    TypeDiodesVdcRipple freqPIV
    Half-wave1Vm/πfVm
    Full-wave (centre-tap)22Vm/π2f2Vm
    Bridge42Vm/π2fVm

    The bridge rectifier is most common because it has the same output as centre-tap but needs no centre-tapped transformer and has lower PIV (peak inverse voltage). A capacitor filter smooths the output: larger C → lower ripple.

    Zener diode operates in reverse breakdown at a well-defined voltage VZ. Used as a voltage regulator:

    Rs = (Vin − VZ) / (IZ + IL)
    Rs = series resistor, IL = load current
    The bridge rectifier's PIV advantage is the reason it dominates. In a centre-tap rectifier, when one diode conducts, the non-conducting diode sees Vm from the conducting half plus Vm from the non-conducting half = 2Vm. In a bridge, each diode only sees Vm.

    6. Clippers and clampers

    Clippers (limiters) cut off part of a waveform. Clampers (DC restorers) shift the entire waveform up or down without changing its shape. These are high-frequency exam questions — the typical format shows a circuit and asks you to draw the output waveform.

    Clipper types:

    • Positive clipper: diode clips the positive portion. With a bias voltage VB, it clips above VB + 0.7.
    • Negative clipper: clips the negative portion. With bias, clips below −(VB + 0.7).
    • Combination clipper: two diodes with biases clip both positive and negative peaks.

    The method for solving any clipper: for each half-cycle, determine if the diode is forward-biased. If yes, the output is clamped to the bias + diode drop. If no, the output follows the input.

    Clamper types:

    • Positive clamper: shifts the waveform up so its negative peak sits at 0 V (or at a bias level).
    • Negative clamper: shifts the waveform down so its positive peak sits at 0 V.

    The clamper works through a capacitor: during the first half-cycle, the capacitor charges to the peak value. Then the capacitor voltage adds to (or subtracts from) the input, shifting the entire waveform. For an ideal clamper (no load), the peak-to-peak output equals the peak-to-peak input.

    The quick rules: a clipper changes the shape (removes peaks). A clamper preserves the shape but moves the DC level. If the exam shows a capacitor in series with the signal and a diode to ground, it is a clamper. If the diode is in series or parallel with the signal path (no coupling capacitor), it is a clipper.

    7. Transistors

    A BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor) is a current-controlled device with three terminals: Base, Collector, Emitter. Two types: NPN (current flows C→E, conventional) and PNP (current flows E→C).

    Three regions of operation:

    RegionBE junctionBC junctionBehaves as
    Cut-offReverseReverseOpen switch (IC ≈ 0)
    ActiveForwardReverseAmplifier (IC = βIB)
    SaturationForwardForwardClosed switch (VCE ≈ 0.2 V)

    The current relationships:

    IE = IC + IB
    IC = β IB = α IE
    α = β/(β+1), β = α/(1−α)
    α ≈ 0.95–0.99, β ≈ 20–500

    Configurations — common base (voltage gain, no current gain), common emitter (both voltage and current gain, most used), common collector/emitter follower (current gain, voltage gain ≈ 1, buffer).

    Biasing: voltage-divider bias is the most stable configuration. The Q-point (operating point) is set on the load line; for a good amplifier, it should be in the middle of the active region.

    FET and MOSFET are voltage-controlled devices. JFET has a channel controlled by a gate-source voltage; MOSFET (enhancement and depletion types) is the building block of digital ICs. Key advantage over BJT: very high input impedance, lower power consumption, easier to fabricate in VLSI.

    For the exam, the most common question is: given a circuit, determine if the BJT is in cut-off, active or saturation. Check VBE: if < 0.5 V → cut-off. If ≥ 0.7 V, check VCE: if > 0.2 V → active, if ≤ 0.2 V → saturation.

    8. Operational amplifiers

    An ideal op-amp has infinite input impedance (no current flows into inputs), zero output impedance, infinite open-loop gain and infinite bandwidth. Two key analysis rules follow:

    • Virtual short: with negative feedback, V+ = V−.
    • No input current: I+ = I− = 0.

    The two fundamental amplifier configurations:

    ConfigGain (Av)InputPhase
    Inverting−Rf/RinTo V− via Rin180° inverted
    Non-inverting1 + Rf/RinTo V+ directlyIn-phase

    Voltage follower: non-inverting with Rf = 0 and Rin = ∞ → gain = 1. Used as a buffer (high impedance in, low impedance out).

    Other common op-amp circuits: summing amplifier (Vout = −Rf(V₁/R₁ + V₂/R₂ + …)), difference amplifier, integrator (Rf replaced by C), differentiator (Rin replaced by C), comparator (no feedback, output swings to ±Vsat).

    CMRR = 20 log₁₀(Ad/Acm) dB
    Higher CMRR → better rejection of common-mode noise
    The two rules (virtual short + no input current) solve every op-amp circuit in the exam. Write KCL at the inverting input node and substitute V− = V+.

    Solved past questions

    10 questions
    S1 Find the equivalent resistance between terminals A and B for the circuit: R₁=6Ω and R₂=3Ω in parallel, in series with R₃=4Ω. standard repeat
    1. Parallel combination of R₁ and R₂
      R12 = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2 Ω
    2. Series with R₃
      Req = R12 + R₃ = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω
    S2 Using KVL, find the current in a circuit with a 24V source, R₁=4Ω, R₂=8Ω in series. Verify with KCL at the junction. standard repeat
    1. Apply KVL around the loop
      −24 + I×4 + I×8 = 0
      12I = 24
      I = 2 A
    2. Voltage drops
      VR1 = 2 × 4 = 8 V
      VR2 = 2 × 8 = 16 V
      Check: 8 + 16 = 24 V ✓
    S3 Find the Thevenin equivalent across RL in a circuit with Vs=20V, R₁=5Ω, R₂=15Ω (voltage divider), RL=10Ω. standard repeat
    1. Remove RL, find Vth (open-circuit voltage across R₂)
      Vth = 20 × 15/(5+15) = 20 × 0.75 = 15 V
    2. Deactivate Vs (short it), find Rth
      Rth = R₁ ∥ R₂ = (5 × 15)/(5 + 15) = 75/20 = 3.75 Ω
    3. Load current
      IL = Vth/(Rth + RL) = 15/(3.75 + 10) = 15/13.75
      = 1.091 A
    S4 A Zener diode has VZ=5.1V and the load draws 20 mA. Input voltage is 12V. Design the series resistor Rs assuming minimum Zener current of 5 mA. standard repeat
    1. Total current through Rs
      Is = IZ + IL = 5 + 20 = 25 mA
    2. Calculate Rs
      Rs = (Vin − VZ) / Is = (12 − 5.1) / 0.025
      = 6.9 / 0.025 = 276 Ω
      Standard value: use 270 Ω (IZ will be slightly above 5 mA)

    Power in Rs: P = I²R = 0.025² × 276 = 0.173 W → use a ¼ W resistor.

    S5 Compare half-wave and full-wave (bridge) rectifiers in terms of Vdc, ripple frequency, and PIV. standard repeat

    Given: Input: Vm sin(ωt), supply frequency f

    ParameterHalf-waveBridge (full-wave)
    Diodes needed14
    VdcVm/π = 0.318 Vm2Vm/π = 0.636 Vm
    Ripple frequencyf2f
    Ripple factor1.21 (121%)0.48 (48%)
    PIV per diodeVmVm
    Efficiency40.6%81.2%
    TransformerSimpleSimple (no centre tap)

    Key advantage of bridge: twice the DC output, lower ripple, same PIV — at the cost of two extra diodes (cheap). The centre-tap rectifier matches the bridge output but requires a centre-tapped transformer and has PIV = 2Vm.

    S6 A series RLC circuit has R=10Ω, L=0.1H, C=100µF. Find the resonant frequency, Q-factor and bandwidth. standard repeat
    1. Resonant frequency
      f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π√(0.1 × 100×10⁻⁶))
      = 1/(2π√(10⁻⁵)) = 1/(2π × 3.162×10⁻³)
      = 1/0.01987 ≈ 50.33 Hz
    2. Q-factor
      Q = ω₀L/R = 2π × 50.33 × 0.1 / 10
      = 31.63 × 0.1 / 10 = 3.163 / 10
      ≈ 3.16
    3. Bandwidth
      BW = f₀/Q = 50.33/3.16 ≈ 15.93 Hz
      Or directly: BW = R/(2πL) = 10/(2π×0.1) = 15.92 Hz ✓
    S7 Draw the output waveform for a positive clipper with a bias of 3V, given a 10V peak sine wave input. Assume Si diode (0.7V drop). standard repeat
    1. Determine the clipping level
      Clip level = Vbias + Vdiode = 3 + 0.7 = 3.7 V
    2. Analyse each half-cycle

      Positive half: when input > 3.7 V, the diode conducts and clamps the output to 3.7 V. When input ≤ 3.7 V, the diode is off and the output follows the input.

      Negative half: the diode is always reverse-biased, so the output follows the input down to −10 V.

    3. Output description

      The output is a sine wave that follows the input from −10 V up to +3.7 V, then is flat at +3.7 V for the portion where the input exceeds 3.7 V. The positive peak is clipped from 10 V to 3.7 V.

    S8 An inverting op-amp has Rin=10kΩ and Rf=100kΩ. Find the gain, and the output for Vin=0.5V. standard repeat
    1. Voltage gain
      Av = −Rf/Rin = −100k/10k = −10
    2. Output voltage
      Vout = Av × Vin = −10 × 0.5 = −5 V
      The negative sign means the output is inverted (180° phase shift).
    S9 An NPN transistor has β=100. Base current is 50 µA. Find IC, IE and α. standard repeat
    1. Collector current
      IC = β × IB = 100 × 50 µA = 5 mA
    2. Emitter current
      IE = IC + IB = 5 + 0.05 = 5.05 mA
    3. Alpha
      α = β/(β+1) = 100/101 = 0.99
      Verify: α = IC/IE = 5/5.05 = 0.99 ✓
    S10 Calculate the power factor and real power for a load drawing 5A from a 230V supply with a phase angle of 37°. standard repeat
    1. Power factor
      pf = cos 37° = 0.8 lagging
    2. Apparent power
      S = V × I = 230 × 5 = 1150 VA
    3. Real power
      P = S × cos φ = 1150 × 0.8 = 920 W
    4. Reactive power
      Q = S × sin φ = 1150 × sin 37° = 1150 × 0.6 = 690 VAR
      Check: √(920² + 690²) = √(846400 + 476100) = √1322500 = 1150 VA ✓

    Practice questions

    10 questions
    P1 Three resistors (10Ω, 15Ω, 30Ω) are connected in parallel to a 30V source. Find the equivalent resistance, total current and individual branch currents. practice
    1. Equivalent resistance
      1/Req = 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/30 = 3/30 + 2/30 + 1/30 = 6/30
      Req = 30/6 = 5 Ω
    2. Total current
      Itotal = V/Req = 30/5 = 6 A
    3. Branch currents
      I₁ = 30/10 = 3 A, I₂ = 30/15 = 2 A, I₃ = 30/30 = 1 A
      Check: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 A ✓ (KCL)
    P2 Find the Norton equivalent for a circuit with Vs=30V, R₁=6Ω in series with the source, R₂=12Ω as the load. practice
    1. Short-circuit current (short the load terminals)
      IN = Vs/R₁ = 30/6 = 5 A
    2. Norton resistance
      RN = R₁ = 6 Ω (looking into terminals with Vs shorted)
    3. Load current
      IL = IN × RN/(RN+RL) = 5 × 6/(6+12) = 30/18
      = 1.667 A
      Verify via Thevenin: Vth=IN×RN=30V, IL=30/18=1.667A ✓
    P3 An RC circuit has R=100kΩ, C=10µF. Find the time constant, and the capacitor voltage after 1s if charged from a 10V source. practice
    1. Time constant
      τ = RC = 100×10³ × 10×10⁻⁶ = 1 s
    2. Voltage at t = 1s = 1τ
      v(1) = 10 × (1 − e⁻¹) = 10 × 0.6321 = 6.321 V
      At 5τ (5s): v = 10 × (1 − e⁻⁵) = 10 × 0.9933 ≈ 9.93 V (essentially fully charged)
    P4 A load of impedance Z = 30 + j40 Ω is connected to a 240V AC supply. Find the current, power factor, real power and reactive power. practice
    1. Impedance magnitude and angle
      |Z| = √(30² + 40²) = √(900 + 1600) = √2500 = 50 Ω
      φ = arctan(40/30) = arctan(1.333) = 53.13°
    2. Current
      I = V/|Z| = 240/50 = 4.8 A
    3. Power factor
      pf = cos 53.13° = R/|Z| = 30/50 = 0.6 lagging
    4. Powers
      S = 240 × 4.8 = 1152 VA
      P = 1152 × 0.6 = 691.2 W
      Q = 1152 × sin 53.13° = 1152 × 0.8 = 921.6 VAR
    P5 Find the maximum power that can be delivered to a load from a source with Vth=48V and Rth=12Ω. What load resistance is needed? practice
    1. Load for maximum power transfer
      RL = Rth = 12 Ω
    2. Maximum power
      Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth) = 48²/(4×12) = 2304/48
      = 48 W
      Efficiency = 50% (half the power is dissipated in Rth)
    P6 Draw the output waveform for a negative clamper with an ideal diode, given a 5V peak sine wave input. practice
    1. First positive half-cycle: capacitor charges

      During the first positive peak, the diode conducts (forward biased). The capacitor charges to Vm = 5 V with the polarity that opposes the input.

    2. Subsequent cycles: capacitor voltage adds

      The output = input − Vcap = input − 5 V (the capacitor holds its charge since the diode prevents discharge).

    3. Output range
      Positive peak: 5 − 5 = 0 V
      Negative peak: −5 − 5 = −10 V

      The waveform shape is preserved, but shifted down so the positive peak sits at 0 V.

    P8 A non-inverting op-amp has Rf=47kΩ and R1=10kΩ. Find the gain and output for Vin=0.8V. practice
    1. Gain
      Av = 1 + Rf/R₁ = 1 + 47k/10k = 1 + 4.7 = 5.7
    2. Output
      Vout = 5.7 × 0.8 = 4.56 V
      In-phase with input (no inversion).
    P9 A BJT CE amplifier has VCC=12V, RC=2kΩ, RB=200kΩ, β=100. Find IB, IC, VCE. Is it in active or saturation? practice
    1. Base current (assume VBE=0.7V)
      IB = (VCC − VBE) / RB = (12 − 0.7) / 200k
      = 11.3/200000 = 56.5 µA
    2. Collector current
      IC = β × IB = 100 × 56.5 µA = 5.65 mA
    3. VCE
      VCE = VCC − IC × RC = 12 − 5.65×10⁻³ × 2000
      = 12 − 11.3 = 0.7 V
    4. Region check

      VCE = 0.7 V > 0.2 V (saturation threshold), so the transistor is in the active region — but barely. It is close to the edge of saturation.

    P10 A summing amplifier has Rf=20kΩ, R₁=10kΩ, R₂=20kΩ. Inputs V₁=2V, V₂=3V. Find Vout. practice
    1. Apply summing amplifier formula
      Vout = −Rf(V₁/R₁ + V₂/R₂)
      = −20k × (2/10k + 3/20k)
      = −20k × (0.0002 + 0.00015)
      = −20k × 0.00035
      = −7 V

      The output is the inverted weighted sum: V₁ gets weight 20k/10k = 2, V₂ gets weight 20k/20k = 1. So Vout = −(2×2 + 1×3) = −7 V.
