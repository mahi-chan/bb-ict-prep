    Focus Writing

    1. Reusable essay structure

    The BB AD (ICT) written section typically asks you to write 300–500 words on an ICT topic. A consistent structure saves planning time under exam pressure.

    The 5-paragraph template:

    1. Opening paragraph (2–3 sentences)

      Hook with a statistic or recent event → one-line definition of the topic → thesis statement previewing your 3 points.

    2. Body paragraph 1 — Technical explanation

      What is it, how does it work? Give the mechanism. Use a concrete example.

    3. Body paragraph 2 — Benefits / applications

      Why does it matter? List 3–4 specific benefits with banking/ICT examples.

    4. Body paragraph 3 — Challenges / risks

      Show balanced thinking. Security risks, cost, skill gap, regulatory hurdles.

    5. Bangladesh context paragraph

      Mention BB initiative, government policy, or BD-specific example. This is the paragraph that scores highest — examiners want to see you connect theory to Bangladesh reality.

    6. Conclusion (2–3 sentences)

      Restate thesis. End with a forward-looking statement about the topic's impact on Bangladesh banking.

    This template works for 90% of focus-writing prompts. Memorise it, and in the exam you only need to fill in the specific content — you never waste time deciding on structure.

    3. Language toolkit

    Pre-built phrases for each section of the essay. Mix and match — don't use the same opener every time.

    Opening hooks:

    • "In an era where digital transformation is reshaping the global financial landscape, [topic] has emerged as a critical enabler for…"
    • "With over 180 million MFS accounts and a rapidly growing digital economy, Bangladesh stands at the threshold of…"
    • "The [year] [event] underscored the urgent need for [topic] in the banking sector."
    • "[Topic] is no longer a futuristic concept — it is a present-day necessity for modern banking operations."

    Transition phrases:

    PurposePhrases
    Adding a pointFurthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally
    ContrastingHowever, Nevertheless, On the other hand, Despite this
    Giving examplesFor instance, A notable example is, This is exemplified by
    Showing causeConsequently, As a result, This leads to, Therefore
    BD contextIn the context of Bangladesh, Bangladesh Bank has taken steps to, Under the Smart Bangladesh Vision

    Closing sentences:

    • "As Bangladesh progresses toward its Smart Bangladesh 2041 vision, [topic] will play a pivotal role in…"
    • "With proper regulatory framework and capacity building, [topic] can transform Bangladesh's banking sector into a global benchmark."
    • "Bangladesh Bank's proactive stance on [topic] positions the country well to harness its benefits while mitigating associated risks."
    Examiners value clear, professional English over flowery language. Short sentences with concrete facts beat long sentences with vague claims. Aim for 15–20 words per sentence on average.

    4. Common pitfalls and scoring

    What examiners look for:

    CriterionWeightWhat scores well
    Technical accuracyHighCorrect definitions, proper terminology, accurate facts
    Bangladesh relevanceHighBB initiatives, BD policies, local examples
    StructureMediumClear paragraphs, logical flow, introduction → body → conclusion
    Balanced viewMediumBenefits AND challenges, not one-sided
    Language qualityMediumClear, concise, professional English
    Specific examplesMediumNamed systems, real statistics, concrete use cases

    Common pitfalls to avoid:

    • Vague generalities: "Technology is very important for banking" — instead, name the specific technology and its specific banking application
    • No BD context: writing only about global trends without connecting to Bangladesh — always include a Bangladesh paragraph
    • One-sided: only listing benefits without mentioning challenges (or vice versa)
    • Incorrect facts: wrong dates, wrong acronym expansions, invented statistics — better to omit than to guess
    • Too short / too long: aim for 300–500 words; below 200 looks incomplete, above 600 wastes time
    • No structure: a single wall of text without paragraphs — use the 5-paragraph template
    Time budget: 5 min planning (pick 3 body points + BD fact)
    15 min writing (3 min per paragraph × 5)
    5 min review (check facts, grammar, completeness)
    Total: ~25 min for a polished 400-word essay

    5. Prompt analysis

    Before writing, decode the prompt to determine what the examiner wants:

    Prompt keywordWhat to writeStructure emphasis
    "Discuss"Cover multiple aspects — definition, benefits, challenges, examplesBalanced body paragraphs
    "Explain"Focus on how it works — mechanism, process, technical detailHeavy on technical body
    "Compare"Side-by-side analysis of two or more thingsUse comparison table + prose
    "Role of X in Y"How X specifically contributes to Y — use cases, impactApplication-focused body
    "Challenges"Problems, risks, obstacles — but still mention mitigationProblem → solution structure
    "Impact on banking"Concrete changes in banking operations, services, securityBefore/after framing
    The single most common exam prompt pattern is "Discuss the role of [emerging tech] in Bangladesh's banking sector." If you prepare 6–8 topic-specific body paragraphs (cloud, AI, blockchain, IoT, MFS, cybersecurity, digital banking, fintech), you can assemble any essay in 5 minutes.

    S1Discuss the role of mobile financial services (MFS) in financial inclusion in Bangladesh.standard repeat

    With over 180 million registered accounts and 1.5 million agents spread across every union of the country, mobile financial services have become the primary engine of financial inclusion in Bangladesh. MFS allows users to perform cash-in, cash-out, person-to-person transfers, bill payments and merchant payments using a basic mobile phone — no bank branch visit required.

    Technically, MFS operates through USSD channels on feature phones and dedicated apps on smartphones. A customer registers with their National ID, completes simplified KYC, and gains immediate access to a mobile wallet. The agent network functions as a distributed cash-handling infrastructure — essentially human ATMs in places where bank branches and ATMs do not exist. Bangladesh Bank regulates all MFS providers, requiring them to operate as bank-led entities with daily transaction limits of BDT 25,000 and monthly limits of BDT 200,000.

    The benefits are substantial. First, MFS has brought over 50% of the adult population into the formal financial system — many for the first time. Second, government social safety-net payments (old age allowance, widow allowance, stipends) are now disbursed via MFS, reducing leakage and corruption. Third, garment workers receive wages digitally, which is safer than carrying cash and creates a financial trail that can eventually qualify them for micro-credit.

    However, challenges remain. Transaction costs, though small, accumulate for low-income users. Agent fraud (overcharging, unauthorized transactions) occurs in remote areas with weak oversight. Digital literacy barriers persist among elderly and rural populations. Additionally, the concentration of the market (bKash alone holds over 60% market share) raises competition concerns.

    Bangladesh Bank has responded proactively through the National Payment Switch (NPS), which enables interoperability between MFS providers and banks — a customer can now send money from bKash to a bank account or from Nagad to Rocket. The introduction of Bangla QR for merchant payments further extends MFS beyond simple P2P transfers into everyday commerce.

    As Bangladesh advances toward its Smart Bangladesh 2041 vision, MFS will remain the cornerstone of financial inclusion, bridging the gap between the formal banking system and the unbanked majority.

    S2Explain the importance of cybersecurity in the banking sector.standard repeat

    The 2016 Bangladesh Bank SWIFT heist, which resulted in the theft of $81 million from the central bank's reserves at the New York Federal Reserve, was a stark reminder that cybersecurity is not optional for the banking sector — it is existential. As banks digitize operations through internet banking, mobile apps and real-time payment systems, their attack surface expands proportionally.

    Banks face a range of cyber threats: phishing attacks targeting employee credentials, ransomware that can lock critical systems, DDoS attacks that disrupt online services, and sophisticated APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) groups that target SWIFT terminals and core banking systems. The financial sector is the most targeted industry globally because a successful breach yields direct monetary gain.

    A robust cybersecurity framework involves multiple layers. Network security (firewalls, IDS/IPS, network segmentation) forms the perimeter. Application security (secure coding, VAPT, WAF) protects customer-facing systems. Data security (encryption at rest and in transit, data classification, DLP) safeguards sensitive information. Identity and access management (MFA, RBAC, privileged access management) controls who can do what. Finally, a Security Operations Centre (SOC) provides 24/7 monitoring and incident response.

    The consequences of failure are severe: direct financial loss, regulatory penalties, customer trust erosion and systemic risk to the entire banking system. A breach at one bank can cascade through payment systems (RTGS, BEFTN) and affect others.

    Bangladesh Bank has issued comprehensive ICT Security Guidelines mandating all scheduled banks to appoint a CISO, conduct annual VAPT through independent auditors, report cyber incidents within 24 hours, and comply with the SWIFT Customer Security Programme. PCI-DSS compliance is required for all card-processing operations. These measures, combined with the Digital Security Act 2018, provide the regulatory backbone for banking cybersecurity in Bangladesh.

    Cybersecurity is no longer an IT cost — it is a business imperative that directly protects depositor funds, national reserves and public confidence in the financial system.

    S3Discuss how cloud computing can benefit the banking sector in Bangladesh.standard repeat

    Cloud computing — the on-demand delivery of computing resources over the internet — is transforming how banks architect their IT infrastructure globally. For Bangladesh's banking sector, where 62 scheduled banks must serve 170 million people with limited IT budgets, cloud computing offers a path to scalable, cost-efficient modernisation.

    The three service models (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS) each serve different banking needs. IaaS provides flexible infrastructure for disaster recovery sites and test environments without massive capital expenditure. PaaS accelerates application development — banks can build and deploy mobile banking apps faster. SaaS delivers ready-made solutions for email, CRM and HR management at a fraction of the on-premise cost.

    Key benefits include elastic scalability (handle transaction spikes during salary days or festivals), reduced capital expenditure (shift from CAPEX to OPEX), improved disaster recovery (cloud-based backup with near-zero RPO), and faster time-to-market for new digital products. Cloud also democratises technology — smaller banks can access the same computing power as larger ones.

    However, significant challenges exist. Data sovereignty is paramount — Bangladesh Bank requires banking data to remain within Bangladesh's borders, limiting public cloud options (few global providers have data centres in Bangladesh). Security concerns around shared infrastructure, regulatory compliance gaps, vendor lock-in risks, and the need for skilled cloud engineers all require careful planning.

    The practical solution for most Bangladeshi banks is a hybrid cloud approach: private cloud (or on-premise) for core banking systems and customer data, with public cloud for non-sensitive workloads like analytics, development/testing and backup. The Bangladesh National Data Centre at Kaliakoir Hi-Tech Park provides a local cloud infrastructure option. Bangladesh Bank is developing cloud-adoption guidelines to provide a regulatory framework.

    With proper governance, cloud computing can significantly enhance the efficiency and resilience of Bangladesh's banking infrastructure.

    S4Discuss the role of AI and machine learning in modern banking.standard repeat

    Artificial intelligence and machine learning are no longer experimental technologies for the banking sector — they are production tools driving critical operations from fraud detection to customer service. AI is the broad goal of creating intelligent systems, while ML is the technique of learning patterns from data without explicit programming.

    In fraud detection, ML models analyse millions of transactions in real time, scoring each for risk. Unlike rigid rule-based systems ("flag if amount > BDT 5 lakh"), ML detects novel fraud patterns by learning normal customer behaviour and flagging anomalies. Banks using ML-based fraud detection report 50–60% fewer false positives and catch emerging fraud types that rules miss.

    Beyond fraud, AI applications span the banking value chain. Chatbots handle 30–40% of routine customer queries (balance, branch location, loan eligibility), reducing call-centre costs. Credit scoring models use alternative data (mobile usage, transaction history) to assess unbanked customers who lack traditional credit history. NLP-powered document processing extracts information from loan applications and KYC forms, cutting processing time from days to hours. Algorithmic trading and robo-advisory services are emerging in wealth management.

    Challenges include the "black box" problem — when ML models reject a loan application, the bank must explain why (regulatory requirement). Data quality is critical — ML models are only as good as their training data. Bias in training data can lead to discriminatory outcomes. High implementation costs and the shortage of data scientists in Bangladesh are practical barriers.

    In Bangladesh, several banks have deployed AI-powered chatbots and fraud detection systems. Bangladesh Bank is exploring AI for regulatory supervision (regtech) and studying CBDC feasibility. The growing MFS transaction data (millions of daily transactions) provides a rich dataset for ML applications in financial inclusion, credit scoring and anomaly detection.

    AI and ML will increasingly define competitive advantage in Bangladesh's banking sector, making early adoption and talent development strategic priorities.

    S5What is blockchain? Discuss its potential applications in banking.standard repeat

    Blockchain is a distributed, immutable ledger technology where transactions are grouped into cryptographically linked blocks, creating a tamper-proof chain maintained by a network of nodes. Each block contains a hash of the previous block — altering any historical transaction would break the chain and be detected immediately.

    The technology operates through consensus mechanisms. Public blockchains like Bitcoin use Proof of Work (computational puzzle-solving), while enterprise blockchains prefer Proof of Stake or PBFT (Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance) for faster finality and lower energy consumption. Smart contracts — self-executing programs on the blockchain — automate agreements when predefined conditions are met.

    Banking applications are particularly promising. Cross-border payments currently take 2–5 days via SWIFT and cost $25–50 per transaction; blockchain-based solutions like Ripple can settle in seconds at a fraction of the cost. Trade finance — a paper-heavy process involving letters of credit, bills of lading and multiple intermediaries — can be digitised on a consortium blockchain, reducing processing from weeks to hours. Shared KYC blockchains allow one bank's customer verification to be trusted by others, eliminating redundant verification. Interbank settlement on a shared ledger can provide real-time gross settlement with full auditability.

    Challenges include scalability (public blockchains process 7–15 transactions per second vs Visa's 65,000), regulatory uncertainty, integration complexity with legacy core banking systems, and energy consumption concerns for PoW-based networks.

    Bangladesh Bank has maintained a clear position: cryptocurrency trading is banned under existing regulations, but blockchain technology itself is recognised as potentially beneficial. The government is exploring blockchain for land record management and supply chain tracking. For banking, consortium blockchains for trade finance are the most likely near-term application.

    Blockchain's potential to reduce costs, increase transparency and eliminate intermediaries makes it a technology that Bangladesh's banking sector must understand and strategically evaluate.

    S6Discuss the digital transformation of Bangladesh's payment systems.standard repeat

    Bangladesh has undergone a remarkable transformation in payment systems over the past decade, moving from a cash-dominated economy to one where electronic payments process billions of taka daily. This transformation, led by Bangladesh Bank, encompasses automated clearing, real-time settlement and mobile payments.

    The backbone of this transformation is three BB-operated systems. BACH (Bangladesh Automated Clearing House) digitised cheque clearing through image truncation — physical cheques no longer travel between banks. BEFTN (Bangladesh Electronic Funds Transfer Network) enables same-day batch electronic transfers for salary disbursement and vendor payments. RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement) handles high-value transfers (minimum BDT 1 lakh) with immediate, irrevocable settlement, eliminating settlement risk for critical transactions.

    The National Payment Switch (NPS) represents the interoperability layer, connecting all banks, MFS providers and payment service providers. NPS enables interbank ATM transactions, cross-platform MFS transfers (bKash-to-Nagad, MFS-to-bank) and the Bangla QR standard for interoperable merchant payments. This interoperability is critical — without it, each payment platform would remain a closed ecosystem.

    MFS has added a massive retail layer with 180 million accounts and 1.5 million agents. Government disbursements, garment worker salaries and remittances increasingly flow through digital channels rather than cash.

    Challenges persist: merchant payment adoption remains low outside urban areas, the digital divide excludes elderly and illiterate populations, cybersecurity threats grow with digitalisation and interoperability between legacy and modern systems requires ongoing investment.

    Bangladesh Bank continues to drive innovation through the Bangla QR standard, regulatory sandbox for fintech testing, CBDC feasibility study, and open banking framework development. EMV migration for all cards, contactless payments, and QR-based merchant acquisition are expanding the acceptance network.

    Bangladesh's payment system transformation is a success story that positions the country well for the Smart Bangladesh 2041 vision of a cashless, inclusive digital economy.

    S7Explain the concept of Internet of Things (IoT) and its impact on banking.standard repeat

    The Internet of Things connects physical devices — sensors, actuators, processors — to the internet, enabling them to collect, exchange and act on data without human intervention. With an estimated 30 billion connected devices worldwide, IoT is reshaping industries including banking and financial services.

    IoT operates through four layers: the perception layer (sensors collecting data), the network layer (transmitting via Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, LoRa or cellular), the processing layer (edge computing or cloud analytics) and the application layer (user dashboards and automated responses). Protocols like MQTT provide lightweight messaging optimised for resource-constrained devices.

    In banking, IoT applications are increasingly practical. Smart ATMs equipped with sensors can monitor cash levels and predict refill schedules, detect physical tampering or card-skimming devices, and report environmental conditions. Wearable payments through NFC-enabled smartwatches and fitness bands are gaining consumer adoption. Collateral monitoring — using GPS and environmental sensors on financed assets like vehicles or warehoused goods — gives lending banks real-time visibility into collateral condition and location.

    Security remains the primary concern. IoT devices are resource-constrained and cannot run heavy encryption. Default credentials on millions of devices created the Mirai botnet, which launched devastating DDoS attacks. Firmware updates across deployed device fleets are logistically challenging. Every connected device is a potential entry point into the bank's network.

    In the Bangladesh context, IoT adoption in banking is nascent but growing. ATM monitoring systems are being upgraded with IoT capabilities. The rollout of 5G and NB-IoT networks will provide the connectivity infrastructure needed for large-scale IoT deployment. BB's data centre guidelines increasingly address IoT device security in bank premises.

    IoT's ability to bridge the physical and digital worlds makes it a natural complement to digital banking — turning physical infrastructure into intelligent, connected assets.

    S8Discuss how big data analytics can improve banking operations.standard repeat

    Banks generate terabytes of data daily — transaction records, customer interactions, ATM logs, internet banking sessions, MFS transfers. Big data analytics transforms this raw data into actionable insights that improve operations, manage risk, and enhance customer experience.

    Big data is characterised by its Volume (millions of daily transactions), Velocity (real-time card swipes and transfers), Variety (structured transactions alongside unstructured emails and call recordings), Veracity (data quality challenges) and Value (extracting profitable insights). Technologies like Hadoop for distributed storage and Apache Spark for in-memory processing enable banks to analyse datasets too large for traditional databases.

    Operational improvements are significant. Fraud detection shifts from static rules to real-time anomaly detection, catching emerging patterns that rules miss. Credit risk modelling incorporates alternative data sources (mobile usage, utility payments) to assess customers without credit history — critical for financial inclusion. Customer segmentation enables targeted product offers, improving conversion rates and reducing marketing waste. AML transaction monitoring analyses millions of transactions to identify suspicious patterns (structuring, rapid transfers, unusual geographies).

    The challenge lies in implementation: data quality (garbage in, garbage out), data silos across departments, shortage of data science talent, privacy concerns under evolving regulations, and high infrastructure costs. Banks must also navigate the distinction between a data warehouse (structured, cleaned data for BI reporting) and a data lake (raw data for advanced analytics) — most modern banks need both.

    In Bangladesh, the MFS ecosystem generates massive transaction datasets ripe for analytics. Banks are beginning to use data analytics for credit scoring of previously unbanked populations and for regulatory reporting to Bangladesh Bank. BFIU uses transaction analytics for AML/CFT surveillance.

    Big data analytics is the bridge between the data banks already have and the intelligence they need to serve customers better, manage risk more effectively, and comply with regulatory requirements.

    S9What is the Smart Bangladesh Vision 2041? How does ICT enable it?standard repeat

    Smart Bangladesh 2041 is the government's vision to transform the country into a knowledge-based, innovation-driven, technology-dependent society by 2041. Building on the Digital Bangladesh 2021 achievement, it rests on four pillars: Smart Citizen, Smart Economy, Smart Government and Smart Society.

    ICT is the enabling infrastructure for each pillar. Smart Citizen requires digital literacy, e-learning platforms, and digital skills training — the a2i (Access to Information) programme has already established 8,500+ digital centres providing government services at the grassroots level. Smart Economy depends on digital financial services (MFS, digital banking, e-commerce), digital trade facilitation and ICT industry growth through Hi-Tech Parks. Smart Government means e-governance, digital service delivery, interoperable government databases and paperless administration. Smart Society encompasses telemedicine, smart transportation, IoT-enabled urban infrastructure and digital environmental monitoring.

    Specific ICT enablers include: nationwide broadband connectivity (submarine cables SEA-ME-WE 4 and 5, fibre backhaul, 5G rollout), the Bangladesh National Data Centre at Kaliakoir, national digital architecture (BNDA), digital identity infrastructure (NID, biometric verification) and cybersecurity frameworks (Digital Security Act 2018, CIRT-BD).

    For banking specifically, Smart Bangladesh means interoperable digital payments (NPS, Bangla QR), financial inclusion through MFS and agent banking, open banking APIs, CBDC exploration and AI-powered financial services. Bangladesh Bank's digital initiatives (BACH, BEFTN, RTGS) are already foundational elements of the smart economy.

    Challenges include the digital divide (rural connectivity, digital literacy), cybersecurity threats growing with digitalisation, data privacy framework gaps and the need for massive human capital development in ICT.

    Smart Bangladesh 2041 is achievable only if ICT infrastructure, digital literacy and regulatory frameworks advance in parallel — technology alone is insufficient without the human capital and governance to deploy it effectively.

    S10Discuss the challenges of digital banking adoption in Bangladesh.standard repeat

    Despite remarkable progress — 180 million MFS accounts, 62 banks offering internet banking, and a national payment switch enabling interoperability — digital banking adoption in Bangladesh faces significant challenges that must be addressed for the Smart Bangladesh 2041 vision to succeed.

    The digital divide is the most fundamental barrier. While urban smartphone penetration exceeds 60%, rural areas rely heavily on feature phones with limited functionality. Digital literacy rates remain low, particularly among elderly populations and women in rural areas. Many customers distrust digital transactions and prefer cash — "I can see and count cash; I cannot see digital money" remains a common sentiment.

    Infrastructure gaps persist. Internet connectivity is unreliable in many rural and char (riverine island) areas. Power outages disrupt both customer access and bank systems. ATM and POS terminal density remains low outside divisional cities — Bangladesh has approximately 12,000 ATMs for 170 million people.

    Cybersecurity threats grow with digitalisation. Phishing, vishing (voice phishing) and social engineering attacks target MFS users. The 2016 SWIFT heist exposed vulnerabilities at the highest level. Many smaller banks lack adequate cybersecurity infrastructure, trained personnel and incident response capabilities.

    Regulatory and operational challenges include legacy system integration (older core banking systems struggle with real-time API-driven services), interoperability gaps between different payment platforms, high customer acquisition costs for digital channels, and the need for a comprehensive data protection law.

    Bangladesh Bank is addressing these challenges through multiple initiatives: NPS for interoperability, Bangla QR for merchant payments, agent banking guidelines for last-mile access, mandatory VAPT and CISO requirements for cybersecurity, and a regulatory sandbox for fintech innovation. The a2i programme's digital centres provide digital literacy training at the grassroots level.

    Overcoming these challenges requires a coordinated effort across regulators, banks, fintech companies and development organizations — digital banking is not just a technology project but a socioeconomic transformation.

    P1Discuss the role of fintech in transforming Bangladesh's banking sector.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: Bangladesh's fintech ecosystem has grown from a single MFS provider to a diverse landscape of payment, lending and regtech solutions
    • Point A (What): Define fintech categories — payment tech, lending tech, regtech, insurtech, wealthtech
    • Point B (Benefits): Financial inclusion, lower costs, faster services, innovation, competition
    • Point C (Challenges): Regulatory gaps, consumer protection, data privacy, systemic risk, talent shortage
    • BD context: BB regulatory sandbox, bank-led MFS requirement, NPS interoperability, CBDC study
    • Close: Fintech supplements (not replaces) the regulated banking system — regulation must keep pace with innovation

    Key facts to include: 180M MFS accounts, bKash/Nagad market dominance, BB's position that fintech must operate through licensed banks, regulatory sandbox launch.

    P2Explain the importance of data privacy and protection in digital banking.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: With millions of digital transactions daily, banks hold the most sensitive personal and financial data of citizens
    • Point A (What): Types of data at risk — PII, financial records, transaction history, biometric KYC data
    • Point B (Why it matters): Customer trust, regulatory compliance, financial crime prevention, reputational risk
    • Point C (How to protect): Encryption, access controls, data classification, privacy-by-design, consent management, data minimisation
    • BD context: Digital Security Act 2018, BB ICT guidelines on data classification, need for comprehensive data protection legislation, BFIU data handling
    • Close: Data protection is not just compliance — it is fundamental to maintaining public confidence in digital banking
    P3Compare traditional banking with digital banking — advantages and disadvantages.practice

    Suggested structure (comparison format):

    • Hook: Banking has evolved from branch-only service to 24/7 digital access
    • Comparison table: Access (branch hours vs 24/7), cost (high overhead vs low marginal cost), reach (urban branches vs nationwide mobile), personalisation (relationship manager vs AI-driven), security (physical vs cyber), human touch (present vs limited)
    • Digital advantages: Convenience, speed, financial inclusion, cost efficiency, data-driven services
    • Digital disadvantages: Digital divide, cyber risk, impersonal, system dependency, job displacement
    • BD context: Agent banking bridges the gap; BB promotes digital while maintaining branch network for complex services
    • Close: The future is hybrid — digital for routine transactions, branches for advisory and complex services
    P4Discuss how Bangladesh Bank can leverage emerging technologies for better regulation.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: Regulators worldwide are adopting "suptech" (supervisory technology) to keep pace with rapidly evolving financial services
    • Point A (AI/ML): Automated anomaly detection across bank reports, natural language processing for compliance document review, predictive models for bank stress testing
    • Point B (Big data): Real-time dashboards of systemic risk indicators, cross-bank transaction analysis for AML/CFT, early warning systems for troubled banks
    • Point C (Blockchain/DLT): Immutable audit trails, regulatory reporting on shared ledger, CBDC for monetary policy transmission
    • BD context: BB already operates BACH/BEFTN/RTGS; BFIU uses transaction analytics; BB studying CBDC; regulatory sandbox tests innovation safely
    • Close: Technology-enabled regulation is more effective, faster and less burdensome for both regulator and regulated entities
    P5What is CBDC? Discuss its potential benefits and risks for Bangladesh.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: Over 130 countries are exploring CBDCs; Bangladesh Bank has initiated a feasibility study for a digital taka
    • Point A (What): Central Bank Digital Currency — government-issued legal tender in digital form; unlike crypto (not decentralised, not volatile, not anonymous)
    • Point B (Benefits): Financial inclusion (offline-capable, no bank account needed), reduced cash handling costs, faster cross-border payments, enhanced monetary policy transmission, programmable money (conditional disbursements)
    • Point C (Risks): Bank disintermediation (people hold CBDC instead of bank deposits → banks lose funding), privacy concerns, technology infrastructure requirements, cybersecurity risks at national scale
    • BD context: BB feasibility study, existing MFS infrastructure as distribution channel, NID as identity layer, comparison with China's e-CNY and India's e-Rupee pilots
    • Close: CBDC design choices (wholesale vs retail, account-based vs token-based) will determine its impact on Bangladesh's financial system
    P6Explain the concept of business continuity planning (BCP) for banks.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: When Cyclone Amphan or COVID-19 disrupted operations, banks with robust BCP maintained services while others struggled
    • Point A (What): BCP ensures critical operations continue during/after disruptions; DR is the IT component; RPO = max data loss, RTO = max downtime
    • Point B (Components): Risk assessment, business impact analysis, DR site (hot/warm/cold), backup strategy, communication plan, testing/drills, staff training
    • Point C (Challenges): Cost (hot sites are expensive), testing disruption, keeping plans updated, staff awareness, vendor dependencies
    • BD context: BB mandates geographically separate DR site (≥30 km), annual DR drills, board-approved BCP, RPO near-zero for core banking
    • Close: BCP is not a document on a shelf — it is a living process that must be tested, updated and ingrained in organizational culture
    P7Discuss the role of 5G technology in future banking services.practice

    Suggested structure:

    • Hook: 5G promises 10 Gbps speeds, 1ms latency and support for 1 million devices per km² — a paradigm shift for connected banking
    • Point A (What): 5G capabilities — eMBB (enhanced mobile broadband), URLLC (ultra-reliable low-latency), mMTC (massive machine-type communications)
