    Meshlinks = 6 × 5 / 2 = 15, ports per device = 5, total ports = 30
    Starlinks = 6, 1 port per device + 6 on the hub
    Ringlinks = 6, 2 ports per device
    The pointmesh grows as n², the others as n — at n = 100 that is 4,950 links against 100
    The n² term is why full mesh is rare and why it is used anyway where it matters. Every mesh link is dedicated, so there is no contention, no shared failure and no way for one device's traffic to be observed by another. That is worth paying for between a handful of core routers or data centres — but at 100 branches it is 4,950 physical links, which is why banks run a hub-and-spoke star to a central site, or MPLS, and accept the single point of failure by making the centre redundant instead.

    3. Physical layer and performance

    Bit rate versus baud rate — a guaranteed question Baud rate (signal rate) = number of signal elements sent per second Bit rate (data rate) = number of bits sent per second Bit rate = Baud rate × log₂(L) where L = number of signal levels Baud rate = Bit rate / number of bits per signal element Bits per symbol: ASK/FSK/BPSK = 1 QPSK = 2 8-PSK = 3 16-QAM = 4 64-QAM = 6 Baud rate ≤ bit rate, always. They are equal only when one symbol carries one bit. Bandwidth requirement depends on BAUD rate, which is why higher-order modulation sends more data through the same bandwidth.
    Channel capacity Nyquist (noiseless): C = 2 × B × log₂(L) bits per second Shannon (noisy): C = B × log₂(1 + S/N) bits per second SNR in decibels: SNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ (S/N) → S/N = 10^(SNR(dB)/10) Signal attenuation: dB = 10 log₁₀ (P₂ / P₁) negative = loss, positive = gain Use Nyquist to choose the number of levels; use Shannon as the hard upper limit. When they disagree, the lower figure wins.
    The combined question, worked

    A telephone line has a bandwidth of 3000 Hz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 30 dB. Find the maximum data rate, and the number of signal levels needed to achieve it. This is the standard two-part question, and it needs both formulas — one after the other, not one instead of the other.

    Step 1 — undo the dBSNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀(S/N) = 30 S/N = 10^(30/10) = 10³ = 1000
    Step 2 — ShannonC = B log₂(1 + S/N) = 3000 × log₂(1001) log₂(1001) ≈ 9.967 C = 3000 × 9.967 = 29,901 bps ≈ 30 kbps
    Step 3 — NyquistC = 2 B log₂(L) 29,901 = 2 × 3000 × log₂(L) log₂(L) = 29901 / 6000 = 4.98 ≈ 5
    Answermaximum ≈ 30 kbps, requiring L = 2⁵ = 32 signal levels
    The two formulas answer different questions, which is why you need both. Nyquist has no noise term at all — taken alone it says you can reach any speed you like by adding more signal levels. Shannon says noise sets a hard ceiling no amount of cleverness passes. So the working is always: Shannon tells you the speed limit, Nyquist tells you what equipment reaches it. And if a question gives you both and Nyquist's answer is higher, the honest answer is Shannon's — you cannot exceed the noise limit by adding levels, you only make the levels too close to tell apart.
    Bit rate and baud rate — the confusion worth killing
    Given2000 baud using 16-QAM (16 levels → log₂16 = 4 bits per symbol)
    Bit rate2000 × 4 = 8000 bps
    Reverse12,000 bps over 16-QAM → baud = 12000 / 4 = 3000 baud
    Why it mattersbandwidth depends on the BAUD rate — 16-QAM triples the data through the same channel a BPSK link uses
    Attenuationpower falls from 10 mW to 5 mW dB = 10 log₁₀(5/10) = 10 × (−0.301) = −3.01 dB
    remember −3 dB = half the power; +3 dB = double. Negative means loss.

    Transmission impairments. Attenuation — loss of energy over distance, corrected by amplifiers (analog) or repeaters (digital). Distortion — different frequency components travel at different speeds and arrive out of phase, changing the signal's shape. Noise — thermal, induced, crosstalk and impulse noise added by the environment.

    4. Delay and latency

    The four delays — the single most examinable formula set Transmission delay Tt = message size (bits) / bandwidth (bps) — time to push all the bits onto the link Propagation delay Tp = distance (m) / propagation speed (m/s) — time for one bit to travel the distance speed ≈ 2 × 10⁸ m/s in copper and fibre, 3 × 10⁸ in vacuum Queuing delay Tq — time spent waiting in router buffers (variable) Processing delay Tpr — header inspection, checksum, routing lookup Total latency = Tt + Tp + Tq + Tpr Round-trip time RTT = 2 × one-way latency ← check your answer against this Bandwidth-delay product = bandwidth × propagation delay = the number of bits "in flight" on the link at once
    Store-and-forward — the step candidates miss

    A router must receive a packet completely before forwarding it. So with N intermediate routers there are N + 1 links, and the transmission delay is paid N + 1 times, while the propagation delay is simply the total distance divided by the speed. Add the processing and queuing delay of each of the N routers.

    The same link, two message sizes, opposite conclusions

    A link of 10 Mbps spanning 2000 km, propagation speed 2 × 10⁸ m/s. First send a 1 MB file:

    TransmissionTt = 8 × 10⁶ bits / 10 × 10⁶ bps = 0.8 s
    PropagationTp = 2 × 10⁶ m / 2 × 10⁸ m/s = 0.01 s
    Total0.81 s — transmission is 98.8% of it. Distance is almost irrelevant.

    Now send a single 1 KB packet over the identical link:

    TransmissionTt = 8192 bits / 10⁷ bps = 0.82 ms
    PropagationTp = 10 ms (unchanged — it never depends on message size)
    Total10.82 ms — propagation is now 92% of it. Bandwidth is almost irrelevant.
    Propagation delay does not depend on how much you send; transmission delay does not depend on how far. That one sentence resolves most confusion in this topic. It also explains a thing everyone has noticed: upgrading from 10 Mbps to 100 Mbps makes a large download ten times faster and makes a web page feel barely different — because the page is dominated by round trips, and no amount of bandwidth shortens the distance to Singapore. It is why CDNs exist: the only way to cut propagation delay is to move the data closer.
    Store-and-forward across routers

    A 1000-bit packet crosses 2 routers to reach its destination — so 3 links — each 1 Mbps, total distance 300 km.

    Per linkTt = 1000 bits / 10⁶ bps = 1 ms
    Paid how often?each router must receive the WHOLE packet before forwarding → 3 links = 3 × 1 ms = 3 ms
    PropagationTp = 3 × 10⁵ m / 2 × 10⁸ m/s = 1.5 ms (total distance, counted once)
    Total3 + 1.5 = 4.5 ms, ignoring queuing and processing

    The trap is symmetry: transmission delay is multiplied by the number of links, propagation delay is not — it is just the total distance over the speed. Counting Tp per hop as well is the single commonest error in this question.

    Bandwidth-delay product, and why stop-and-wait collapses
    Bits in flightBDP = bandwidth × Tp = 10⁷ × 0.01 = 100,000 bits = 12.5 KB
    12.5 KB is "on the wire" at any instant — sent but not yet arrived
    a = Tp / Tt10 ms / 0.82 ms = 12.2
    Efficiency1 / (1 + 2a) = 1 / (1 + 24.4) = 1 / 25.4 = 3.9%
    Effective rate0.039 × 10 Mbps ≈ 394 kbps out of 10 Mbps
    Optimal window1 + 2a = 25.4 → 26 frames in flight to keep the link busy
    96% of a 10 Mbps link, wasted, with nothing broken. Stop-and-wait sends one frame and then waits a full round trip doing nothing — 0.82 ms of work followed by 20 ms of silence. The link is not slow, congested or faulty; the protocol is idle. This single calculation is the entire motivation for sliding-window protocols, and the window size that fixes it is exactly the bandwidth-delay product expressed in frames. A question asking "why is my throughput so far below my bandwidth?" is nearly always asking for this.
    Throughput = actual bits delivered per second (≤ bandwidth, often well below) Efficiency (stop-and-wait) = Tt / (Tt + 2Tp) = 1 / (1 + 2a), where a = Tp / Tt Effective throughput = efficiency × bandwidth

    Look at bits 3 and 4 in NRZ-L — that flat stretch is the whole problem. The receiver has its own clock, and clocks drift. If the line is level for 40 zeros the receiver has no event to resynchronise on, and may count 39 or 41. Manchester guarantees a transition in the middle of every bit, so the clock is recovered from the data itself and drift can never accumulate. The bill for that guarantee is exactly double: two signal elements per bit, so 10 Mbps Ethernet needs 20 Mbaud of bandwidth.

    AMI solves a different problem. Alternating each 1 between + and − means the average voltage stays at zero — no DC component — which matters because transformers and capacitively-coupled circuits simply cannot pass DC. But AMI still goes flat during a run of zeros, which is why B8ZS and HDB3 exist: they deliberately insert a code violation the receiver recognises and removes, buying synchronisation without breaking the DC balance.

    Manchester is used by classic 10 Mbps Ethernet; differential Manchester by Token Ring. Both trade bandwidth for guaranteed clock recovery — their signal rate is twice their bit rate.

    PCM — analog to digital Three steps: SAMPLING → QUANTIZATION → ENCODING Nyquist sampling theorem: fs ≥ 2 × f(max) Bit rate = sampling rate × bits per sample Quantization levels L = 2^(bits per sample) Quantization error decreases by ~6 dB per extra bit Telephone voice example: bandwidth 4 kHz → fs = 8000 samples/s 8 bits per sample bit rate = 8000 × 8 = 64,000 bps = 64 kbps ← the DS0 channel

    Run the same three steps on CD-quality audio and the numbers explain a familiar file size:

    Step 1 — samplehuman hearing reaches 20 kHz → fs ≥ 40 kHz; the CD standard uses 44,100 Hz
    Step 2 — quantize16 bits per sample → L = 2¹⁶ = 65,536 levels
    Step 3 — encode44,100 × 16 × 2 channels = 1,411,200 bps
    Answer1.41 Mbps — so one minute of CD audio is about 10.6 MB
    Why 8 kHz for a telephone and 44.1 kHz for music. Nothing about the sampler differs — only the bandwidth being captured. Telephony deliberately throws away everything above about 3.4 kHz, because speech remains perfectly intelligible without it, and sampling at twice 4 kHz costs 64 kbps instead of 1.41 Mbps. That is a 22× saving, and it is why a single voice channel has been 64 kbps since the 1960s. Sample below twice the maximum frequency and you get aliasing: high frequencies are reconstructed as spurious low ones, which is not distortion you can filter out afterwards — the information is already gone.

    Delta modulation sends one bit per sample recording only whether the signal rose or fell — far simpler than PCM but subject to slope overload and granular noise. Serial transmission sends one bit at a time on one wire (cheap, used over distance); parallel sends n bits at once (fast but suffers skew, so it is confined to short internal buses). Asynchronous transmission frames each character with start and stop bits; synchronous sends a continuous bit stream with a shared clock, which is far more efficient for bulk data.

    Constellation diagrams — where "bits per symbol" comes from

    Each dot is one symbol the transmitter can send: its distance from the origin is the amplitude, its angle is the phase. Count the dots and take log₂ — that is the bits per symbol.

    The crowding is the trade. Going from QPSK to 16-QAM doubles the data through the same bandwidth — but the dots are now four times closer together, so a noise spike that was harmless in QPSK is enough to push a received point across into its neighbour's territory and produce a wrong symbol. That is exactly why a WiFi link drops from 256-QAM to 64-QAM to QPSK as you walk away from the access point: it is trading speed for the margin it no longer has. Shannon's limit is the formal statement of the same fact — you may keep adding constellation points, but past a certain density the noise decodes them for you.

    For analog data on an analog carrier the equivalents are AM, FM and PM. A modem modulates digital data onto an analog carrier at the sending end and demodulates at the receiving end.

    Four sources, each producing 1 kbps, are multiplexed by synchronous TDM taking 1 bit per slot.

That is the trade in one line: synchronous TDM wastes bandwidth on idle sources, statistical TDM spends bandwidth on addresses.     7. Switching

    Switching is how data gets from a sender to a receiver when they are not directly connected. The two families differ in one decision: whether the path is reserved in advance or chosen as you go. Put numbers on the same transfer and the consequences fall out.

    One message, three ways

    Send a 3000-bit message from A to B through 2 intermediate switches — so 3 links, each 1 Mbps. Circuit setup takes 100 ms. Propagation and processing are negligible.

    Circuit switchingsetup 100 ms, then the bits flow down a dedicated path with no store-and-forward 100 + (3000 / 10⁶) = 100 + 3 = 103 ms
    Packet, 1 × 3000 bitsno setup, but each switch must receive it all before forwarding 3 links × 3 ms = 9 ms
    Packet, 3 × 1000 bits(links + packets − 1) × 1 ms = (3 + 3 − 1) × 1 = 5 ms

    That last line deserves the picture, because splitting the message made it faster, which is not obvious:

    Splitting a message into packets makes it arrive sooner, and this is why packets exist. With one big message, links 2 and 3 sit idle for the entire time link 1 is transmitting — the pipeline is never full. Cutting the message into three lets the tail of the message enter link 1 while its head is already on link 3. The general result is (N + P − 1) × Tt for N links and P packets, so as P grows the total approaches the time for a single link plus a constant. It does not shrink forever: each packet carries its own header, so past some point the overhead outweighs the pipelining, which is what sets a real MTU.

    Notice also that circuit switching lost badly here (103 ms against 5) because the message was short. Its 100 ms setup is paid once — for a two-hour phone call it is nothing, and the reserved path then gives constant, jitter-free delay that packet switching cannot promise. Short and bursty favours packets; long and steady favours circuits.

    Framing. Character (byte) stuffing inserts an escape byte before any flag byte appearing in the data. Bit stuffing inserts a 0 after five consecutive 1s so the flag pattern 01111110 can never occur inside a frame; the receiver removes it automatically.

    CRC — the method to write out 1. Generator polynomial of degree n → append n zeros to the data 2. Divide the extended data by the generator using MODULO-2 (XOR) arithmetic — no carries, no borrows; subtraction is XOR 3. The n-bit remainder is the CRC; transmit data + CRC 4. The receiver divides the whole received frame by the same generator. Remainder 0 → accept. Remainder ≠ 0 → error detected Rule for the quotient bit: if the leading bit of the current window is 1, XOR with the generator; if it is 0, XOR with zeros. Then shift in the next bit. Common generators: CRC-8, CRC-16 = x¹⁶+x¹⁵+x²+1, CRC-32 (Ethernet)

    The receiver's check. It divides the whole received 14-bit frame 11010110111110 by the same 10011. If nothing was corrupted the remainder is 0000 and the frame is accepted. Any non-zero remainder means an error was detected — note detected, not located: CRC cannot tell you which bit went wrong, only that something did.

    Why appending the zeros works. Adding n zeros multiplies the data by xⁿ, which shifts it left and leaves exactly n empty low-order places. The remainder of that division is by definition smaller than the generator, so it fits precisely into those places. The transmitted frame is therefore data·xⁿ + remainder, and in modulo-2 arithmetic adding the remainder is the same as subtracting it — which makes the whole frame an exact multiple of the generator. That is the entire reason the receiver's remainder should be zero, and it is worth being able to say in one sentence.
    Hamming code — encode and correct Number of parity bits r for m data bits: 2ʳ ≥ m + r + 1 Parity bits sit at positions 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … (powers of 2); data fills the rest. Parity bit at position 2^k checks every position whose binary form has bit k set: p1 (pos 1) checks 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 … (1 on, 1 off) p2 (pos 2) checks 2, 3, 6, 7, 10, 11 … (2 on, 2 off) p4 (pos 4) checks 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 13 … (4 on, 4 off) p8 (pos 8) checks 8–15, 24–31 … To correct: recompute each parity check on the received word. Write the failing checks as a binary number (c8 c4 c2 c1) — that number IS the position of the erroneous bit. Syndrome 0 means no error. Flip that bit to correct it.

    The syndrome is not a code you look up — it is the position, written in binary. That is the whole elegance of Hamming's scheme and it comes straight from where the parity bits sit. Position 7 is 0111 in binary, so bit 7 is included in exactly the checks for the 1s in that pattern: p1, p2 and p4, but not p8. Corrupt bit 7 and precisely those three checks fail, so the failure pattern spells out 0111. Every position has a unique binary pattern, so every single-bit error produces a unique syndrome — and syndrome 0000 means no check failed at all.

    This also shows the limit. Two flipped bits produce the XOR of two patterns, which is some third valid-looking position, and the code "corrects" an innocent bit — making things worse. Basic Hamming detects and corrects one error; adding a single overall parity bit (SECDED) lets it detect two, though still correct only one.

    Minimum frame size, worked

    A CSMA/CD network runs at 100 Mbps over a 1 km cable with signal speed 2 × 10⁸ m/s. What is the smallest frame allowed?

    PropagationTp = 1000 m / 2 × 10⁸ m/s = 5 µs (worst case, end to end)
    The conditionthe sender must still be transmitting when the collision gets back Tt ≥ 2 × Tp = 10 µs
    Convert to bitsL = B × Tt = 100 × 10⁶ × 10 × 10⁻⁶
    AnswerL(min) = 1000 bits = 125 bytes

    Rerun it at 10 Mbps on the same cable and L(min) falls to 100 bits. Speed and minimum frame size move together, because the faster you transmit, the sooner you finish — and finishing before the collision news arrives is exactly the failure being prevented.

    Why a station that finishes too early is a disaster, not an inconvenience. Suppose A sends a 20-bit frame and a collision happens at the far end. A has already stopped transmitting and moved on; when the corrupted signal arrives it sees nothing, because CSMA/CD only detects collisions while transmitting. So A believes the frame was delivered and never retransmits — a silent, permanent loss that the data link layer was specifically built to prevent. The minimum frame size is not an efficiency tuning knob; it is what makes collision detection work at all. Frames shorter than it are padded.

    Controlled access: reservation (stations claim slots in advance), polling (a primary station asks each secondary in turn — simple but the primary is a single point of failure), and token passing (a token circulates and only its holder may transmit — collision-free and deterministic, which is why it suited industrial control).

    Collision domains — the column everyone gets wrong

    A hub is an electrical repeater: a bit arriving on any port is copied to every other port, so if A and C transmit at once their signals overlap and both are destroyed. All four share one domain, only one may talk at a time, and the 100 Mbps is divided among them.

    A switch reads the destination MAC address and forwards only out the port where that address lives. A→B and C→D can therefore happen simultaneously without interfering, each at the full 100 Mbps. With full duplex on each port, collisions cannot occur at all — which is why CSMA/CD is effectively switched off on a modern LAN.

    The switch did not change the broadcast domain, and that is the point of the router. A frame addressed to FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF — an ARP request, a DHCP Discover — is flooded out every port by a switch, exactly as a hub would. So all four PCs remain in one broadcast domain no matter how many switches you add, and a broadcast storm propagates through the whole switched network. Only a router stops it, because it does not forward layer-2 broadcasts. Hence the exam answer: a switch divides collision domains, a router divides broadcast domains, and a VLAN is the way to get a switch to do the second job by making each VLAN its own broadcast domain.

    The one-line answer to "what is the difference between a switch and a router": a switch breaks up collision domains and forwards by MAC address within one network; a router breaks up broadcast domains and forwards by IP address between networks.

    MAC addressing. 48 bits, written as six hex octets, burned into the NIC — the first 24 bits are the OUI identifying the manufacturer. FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF is the broadcast address. ARP resolves a known IP address to an unknown MAC by broadcasting a request and caching the reply; RARP (now replaced by DHCP) did the reverse.

    VLAN partitions one physical switch into several logical broadcast domains, so departments can be separated without new cabling; traffic between VLANs must pass through a router or layer-3 switch. Spanning Tree Protocol (STP, IEEE 802.1D) prevents the broadcast storms that redundant switch links would otherwise cause, by electing a root bridge and blocking the ports that would form loops, while keeping them ready as backups.

    12. Application layer

    DNS resolves names to addresses through a hierarchy: root → top-level domain (.com, .bd) → authoritative name server. In an iterative query each server returns a referral and the resolver chases it; in a recursive query the server does the chasing and returns the final answer. Caching with a TTL keeps the root from being overwhelmed.

    Resolving one name, hop by hop

    Type www.bb.org.bd into a browser. Read the name right to left — that is the order DNS resolves it in.

    1. Check the caches first

      Browser cache → OS resolver cache → the hosts file. Most lookups stop here and never touch the network at all.

    2. Ask the local resolver

      The host sends one recursive query to its configured resolver — the ISP's, or a public one — meaning "do the work and come back with the answer". Everything below is the resolver's job.

    3. Root server: "where is .bd?"

      The root knows nothing about bb.org.bd. It returns a referral: the name servers for .bd. This is an iterative query — a referral, not an answer.

    4. .bd servers: "where is org.bd?"

      Another referral, down to the org.bd name servers.

    5. org.bd servers: "where is bb.org.bd?"

      A referral to the authoritative name servers for the zone bb.org.bd — the ones the domain owner actually runs.

    6. Authoritative server: the answer at last

      It holds the zone file, so it returns the A record for www.bb.org.bd with an IPv4 address, plus a TTL.

    7. Cache and return

      The resolver caches the answer for its TTL and hands the address to the browser, which can now open a TCP connection. The next user asking for the same name is served from that cache.

    Recursive and iterative are not two kinds of DNS server — they are the two halves of one lookup. Your host makes exactly one recursive query and waits. The resolver then makes several iterative queries, each returning a referral rather than an answer. Root servers deliberately refuse recursion: if every one of the world's clients could ask the root to do the chasing, thirteen root server clusters would collapse instantly. Pushing the work outward to thousands of resolvers, each caching aggressively, is what makes the system scale — which is also why a DNS change with a 24-hour TTL takes a day to propagate.

    The DMZ, drawn
    The DMZ exists to make a compromise survivable, not to prevent one. A public web server must accept connections from anyone, so it is the most likely thing to be broken into — that risk cannot be designed away. What the second firewall changes is what an attacker gets. Standing on the web server, they are still outside the internal network, and the inner firewall permits only narrow, specific traffic inward, typically one database port. The web server is never allowed to open arbitrary connections to the core banking network. That is defence in depth: assume the outer layer falls, and make sure that is not the same as losing everything.

    A DMZ is a subnet between two firewalls holding the servers that must be reachable from the internet — web, mail, DNS — so that a compromise there does not reach the internal network. IDS detects and alerts; IPS sits inline and blocks. A VPN creates an encrypted tunnel across a public network — site-to-site joins two offices, remote access connects one user; IPSec provides it at layer 3 in transport mode (payload only) or tunnel mode (the whole original packet), using AH for integrity and ESP for encryption.

    14. Wireless and modern networking

    Radio breaks an assumption wired Ethernet relies on: that every station can hear every other one. Once that fails, carrier sensing stops working — and the standard exam question is why WiFi uses CSMA/CA rather than CSMA/CD.

    The hidden terminal problem

    A listens, hears nothing (C is out of its range), and transmits. C does exactly the same at the same moment. Both frames arrive at B and are destroyed. Neither A nor C ever finds out, because a radio cannot listen on the frequency it is transmitting on — the outgoing signal is billions of times stronger than any incoming one. This is why collision detection is physically impossible on wireless, and why the D in CSMA/CD becomes an A for avoidance.

    The RTS/CTS handshake, which fixes it
    StepWhoSendsEffect
    1A → BRTS "may I send, for 5 ms?"Short frame; a collision here is cheap
    2B → allCTS "yes, A may — everyone else stay quiet 5 ms"C hears this, because C is in B's range
    3A → Bthe data frameC stays silent for the announced duration
    4B → AACKNo ACK ⇒ assume collision and retry
    The CTS is the trick, and it is broadcast for a reason. A cannot reach C, but B can reach both — so B's CTS carries the reservation to exactly the station that could not otherwise know. Every station receiving it sets a network allocation vector, a countdown during which it will not transmit, so the medium is reserved without anyone needing to hear the actual data. This is also why WiFi needs an explicit ACK for every frame while Ethernet does not: with no collision detection, silence is the only evidence of failure available.

The sender writes the message in a Mail User Agent (MUA) — Outlook, Thunderbird or a webmail interface — giving the recipient address, subject and body, plus any attachments encoded by MIME (Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions), which is what allows non-ASCII text, images and files inside a protocol originally designed for 7-bit text.

    3. Routing. The sender's MTA extracts the domain from the recipient address and performs a DNS MX lookup on it to find the receiving mail server, choosing the record with the lowest preference value.

    4. Relay — SMTP, port 25. The sender's MTA opens a TCP connection to the recipient's MTA and pushes the message across. If the destination is unreachable the message is queued and retried for hours or days before a bounce is returned — SMTP is a store-and-forward protocol.

    5. Delivery. The receiving MTA hands the message to a Mail Delivery Agent (MDA), which writes it into the recipient's mailbox, applying spam filtering and rules on the way.

    6. Retrieval — POP3 (port 110) or IMAP (port 143). The recipient's MUA pulls the message from the mailbox. SMTP cannot do this step, because the recipient's machine is often switched off or behind NAT and cannot accept an incoming push.

    Why three protocols and not one

    SMTP is a push protocol: the sender initiates and the message is delivered to a server that is always on. The final hop cannot work that way, because the recipient's device is intermittently connected. So the last leg must be a pull: the client connects when it is ready and fetches what is waiting. That single asymmetry is the reason mail needs both kinds of protocol.

    The one-line distinction

    Flow control protects the receiver from being overwhelmed by a fast sender. Congestion control protects the network from being overwhelmed by all senders together. One is an end-to-end courtesy between two hosts; the other is a shared responsibility towards the routers in between.

    The mechanism that ties them together

    Sender's effective window = min( rwnd , cwnd ) rwnd — receiver window — advertised by the receiver in every ACK cwnd — congestion window — computed by the sender from observed loss The sender is limited by whichever constraint is tighter at that moment.

    1. Flow control in detail. Every ACK the receiver sends carries a 16-bit window size field stating how many further bytes it can accept. The sender may have at most that many unacknowledged bytes outstanding. If the receiving application stops reading, the buffer fills and the advertised window shrinks to zero, which silences the sender completely. The sender then sends periodic window probes so that a lost window update does not deadlock the connection.

      Two refinements worth naming: Silly Window Syndrome is the pathology in which tiny window updates cause tiny segments, wasting bandwidth on headers; it is avoided by Clark's solution at the receiver (do not advertise a window until a worthwhile amount of space is free) and Nagle's algorithm at the sender (do not send a small segment while an earlier one is unacknowledged).

    2. Congestion control in detail. No router tells the sender it is congested, so the sender must infer it. The standard signal is packet loss, on the reasoning that a wired link rarely corrupts a packet, so a loss almost certainly means a full router queue.

      Slow start cwnd = 1 MSS, DOUBLES every RTT until cwnd ≥ ssthresh Congestion avoidance cwnd increases by 1 MSS per RTT — linear Timeout ssthresh = cwnd / 2 ; cwnd = 1 MSS ; slow start again 3 duplicate ACKs fast retransmit, then fast recovery: ssthresh = cwnd / 2 ; cwnd = ssthresh ; stay in avoidance

      This is AIMD — additive increase, multiplicative decrease. It is deliberately asymmetric: probe upwards gently, retreat sharply. That asymmetry is what makes competing TCP flows converge to a fair share of a bottleneck, and it is the reason the internet does not collapse under load.

    3. Why both are necessary — the failure cases.

      ScenarioWhat goes wrong
      Flow control only — a bank server sends a large report to a laptop across a congested WAN The laptop advertises a large window because it has plenty of memory, so the server transmits at full rate. The bottleneck is a router in the middle, whose queue overflows. Packets are lost, retransmitted, lost again. Throughput collapses even though neither endpoint is overloaded
      Congestion control only — the network is idle but the receiver is a small embedded device The sender sees no loss, so cwnd grows without limit. The receiver's small buffer overflows and it discards segments. The sender interprets the resulting loss as congestion and backs off — the right action for the wrong reason, and much slower than simply being told the buffer was full

      Neither mechanism can substitute for the other because they address different bottlenecks and receive different information. Flow control has an explicit, accurate signal from the one party that knows the answer. Congestion control has only inference, but it is the only mechanism with a view of the shared path.

    Flow control is receiver-driven, explicit, and implemented by the advertised window rwnd; it stops the receiver's buffer overflowing. Congestion control is sender-driven, inferred from loss, and implemented by the congestion window cwnd under AIMD; it stops the routers' queues overflowing. The sender always transmits at min(rwnd, cwnd).

    (c) Encapsulation and decapsulation

    See the diagram in notes §2. Trace a browser request from Host A to a web server on another network:

    1. Application. The browser produces an HTTP GET request — just data at this point.

    2. Transport. TCP prepends a header with source port (say 51000), destination port 80, sequence and acknowledgement numbers, flags and window size. The unit is now a segment.

    3. Network. IP prepends a header with the source and destination IP addresses, TTL and protocol number 6. The unit is now a packet.

    4. Data link. Ethernet prepends a header with source and destination MAC addresses — the destination MAC being the default gateway's, not the server's, because the server is on another network — and appends a CRC trailer. The unit is now a frame.

    5. Physical. The frame is transmitted as a stream of bits encoded as voltages or light pulses.

    6. At each router. The frame is decapsulated up to layer 3. The router reads the destination IP, consults its routing table, decrements the TTL, recomputes the header checksum, and then builds a completely new layer-2 frame with new source and destination MAC addresses for the next hop.

      The point that distinguishes a good answer

      The IP addresses stay the same end to end; the MAC addresses change at every hop. Layer-3 addressing is global and identifies the ultimate endpoints; layer-2 addressing is local and identifies only the two ends of the current link.

    7. At the destination. Each layer strips its own header and passes the payload up — decapsulation. The NIC checks the CRC and the destination MAC; IP checks the header checksum and the destination address; TCP checks the checksum, reorders segments by sequence number and delivers a clean byte stream to port 80; the web server reads the HTTP request.

    The governing principle is peer-to-peer communication: each layer on the sender talks logically to the same layer on the receiver, using the header it added, and is oblivious to what the layers below do. That independence is what allows Ethernet to be replaced by WiFi, or IPv4 by IPv6, without rewriting the browser.

    OSI has 7 layers with PDUs bit, frame, packet, segment and data; TCP/IP compresses them into 4 by merging layers 5–7 into Application and layers 1–2 into Network Access. Encapsulation adds one header per layer on the way down and decapsulation strips one per layer on the way up; the IP addresses persist end to end while the MAC addresses are rewritten at every hop.

    Why termination needs four. A close in one direction does not imply a close in the other — a host may have finished sending but still be receiving. So each side must send its own FIN and receive its own ACK:

    1. A → B FIN A has no more data to send 2. B → A ACK B acknowledges; the connection is now HALF-CLOSED — B may still send data to A 3. B → A FIN B has now also finished 4. A → B ACK A acknowledges A then waits 2 × MSL in TIME_WAIT before releasing the port, so that a delayed duplicate segment from this connection cannot be mistaken for data belonging to a new connection using the same four-tuple.

    Unlike the handshake, B's ACK and FIN usually cannot be combined, because B may still have data to send between them — that is exactly what the half-closed state is for.

    (a) CSMA/CD and the minimum frame size

    Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection. A station listens before transmitting (carrier sense). If the medium is idle it transmits, and it keeps listening while transmitting. If it detects another signal, a collision has occurred: it aborts immediately, sends a 32-bit jam signal so every station notices, and waits a random time before retrying. The wait uses truncated binary exponential backoff — after the nth collision the station waits a random multiple of the slot time chosen from 0 … 2ⁿ − 1, capped at n = 10, and gives up after 16 attempts.

    Deriving the condition

    The whole scheme fails if a station finishes transmitting before a collision can reach it — it would then believe the frame was delivered. The worst case is two stations at opposite ends: A starts, and just before A's signal arrives, B senses idle and starts too. B detects the collision almost at once, but A only learns of it when B's signal completes the return trip.

    A must still be transmitting after one round trip: Transmission time ≥ 2 × propagation time L / B ≥ 2 × d / v L(min) = 2 × B × d / v bits The quantity 2 × Tp is called the SLOT TIME.
    Given
    • B = 10 Mbps = 10 × 10⁶ bits/s

    • v = 2 × 10⁸ m/s

    1. One-way propagation time.

      Tp = d / v = 2000 / (2 × 10⁸) = 1 × 10⁻⁵ s = 10 µs

    2. Slot time (round trip).

      2 Tp = 2 × 10 µs = 20 µs

    3. Minimum frame size.

      L(min) = B × 2Tp = 10 × 10⁶ bits/s × 20 × 10⁻⁶ s = 200 bits = 25 bytes

    (b) Why the standard is 64 bytes

    IEEE 802.3 fixes the slot time at 51.2 µs for 10 Mbps Ethernet. That figure covers the maximum permitted 2500 m span plus the latency of up to four repeaters, transceivers and the jam signal — considerably more than the bare 2 × d / v of a single 2500 m cable. L(min) = 10 × 10⁶ × 51.2 × 10⁻⁶ = 512 bits = 64 bytes Any frame shorter than 64 bytes must be padded. A frame received that is shorter than 64 bytes is a "runt" and is discarded as a collision fragment.

    (b) Bit rate and baud rate

    The distinction

    Baud rate counts signal elements (symbols) per second — it determines the bandwidth the signal occupies. Bit rate counts bits per second — it determines the useful data throughput. Higher-order modulation packs more bits into each symbol, raising the bit rate without raising the baud rate or the bandwidth. That is the entire business of modem design.

    Bit rate = baud rate × number of bits per signal element Bits per element = log₂(L), where L is the number of distinct signal levels

    1. 16-QAM at 2000 baud.

      16-QAM → L = 16 → bits per symbol = log₂ 16 = 4 Bit rate = 2000 × 4 = 8000 bps = 8 kbps

    2. 8-QAM carrying 3000 bps.

      8-QAM → L = 8 → bits per symbol = log₂ 8 = 3 Baud rate = bit rate / bits per symbol = 3000 / 3 = 1000 baud

    3. Quick reference.

    Ethernet's CRC-32 is far stronger than this sum, so the question is fair. The answer is that the CRC protects a frame on one link and is stripped and recomputed at every router.     (c) Why IPv6 pushed fragmentation to the sender

    Router fragmentation is expensive in exactly the place you can least afford it. A router forwarding millions of packets a second has to allocate buffers, split the payload, generate new headers and recompute checksums — all in the fast path. Worse, the destination cannot reassemble until every fragment arrives, so losing one fragment destroys the entire datagram and the whole thing must be resent. Fragmentation also frustrates firewalls, since only the first fragment carries the TCP port numbers they need to filter on.

    IPv6 removes the fragment fields from the base header entirely. A router that receives a packet too large for the next link simply drops it and returns an ICMPv6 "Packet Too Big" message stating the MTU it can accept. The sending host lowers its packet size and retries — Path MTU Discovery. The cost is one round trip at the start of a connection; the benefit is that routers stay simple and fast, and every IPv6 link is required to support at least 1280 bytes so the process always converges.

    The thing to be clear about is that two different kinds of query are happening in the same lookup. The client asks its resolver one recursive question and waits for a final answer. The resolver then asks a series of iterative questions, each of which returns not the answer but a pointer to who to ask next.

    1. The browser asks the operating system, which asks the resolver

      The stub resolver on the client machine sends one recursive query to the configured DNS server — usually the ISP's or the bank's own. Recursive means "do not send me a referral, go and find the answer". The client then waits. Before any of this, the OS checks its own cache and the hosts file; we are told both are empty.

    2. The resolver asks a root server

      The resolver has the thirteen root server addresses built in. The root knows nothing about bb.org.bd specifically, but it knows who runs .bd, so it returns NS records for the .bd name servers — a referral, not an answer. This is an iterative query.

    3. The resolver asks the .bd TLD server

      Same pattern one level down. The .bd server refers the resolver to the name servers authoritative for bb.org.bd, again as NS records, usually with their IP addresses attached as "glue" so no second lookup is needed just to find them.

    4. The resolver asks the authoritative server

      This server holds the actual zone file and returns the A record — the IPv4 address — or an AAAA record for IPv6. Now there is a real answer.

    5. The resolver caches and replies

      The resolver stores the answer for the duration of its TTL and returns it to the client. Every subsequent user of that resolver gets the address immediately. Caching is what makes the system survivable — without it the thirteen root servers would field every lookup on earth.

    (c) UDP, and when TCP takes over

    A DNS query and its answer both fit comfortably in a single small packet, and the lookup sits directly in the path of every connection a user makes. Setting up a TCP connection would cost a round trip before the question was even asked, roughly tripling the latency of something that happens constantly. UDP sends the question in one packet and receives the answer in another; if it is lost, the resolver simply asks again, which is cheaper than a handshake would have been.

    DNS switches to TCP in three situations: when a response exceeds 512 bytes and the truncated (TC) bit is set — which is common with DNSSEC signatures — for zone transfers between a primary and secondary server, where reliability genuinely matters, and for the modern encrypted transports DNS-over-TLS and DNS-over-HTTPS, which are TCP-based by construction.

    One recursive query from client to resolver, then three iterative queries from the resolver to the root, the .bd TLD and the authoritative server, which finally returns the A record. UDP is used for speed; TCP takes over for responses above 512 bytes, zone transfers, and encrypted DNS.

    (a) Round trips for 9 objects

    Non-persistent HTTP opens a fresh TCP connection for every object and closes it afterwards. Each object therefore costs two round trips: one for the TCP handshake, one for the request and response.

    Without pipelining, persistent HTTP still needs one round trip per image, giving 1 + 9 = 10 RTT — better than 18, but the large win comes from issuing the image requests without waiting for each response. On a link with a 50 ms RTT the difference is 900 ms against 150 ms, which is the difference between a page that feels slow and one that does not.

    HTTP/1.1 pipelining was fragile in practice because responses had to return in order, so one slow object blocked the rest — head-of-line blocking. HTTP/2 fixed it properly with true multiplexing over a single connection, and HTTP/3 moved to QUIC over UDP to remove the same problem at the TCP layer.

    (b) The TLS handshake

    1. Client hello

      The browser sends the TLS versions it supports, a list of cipher suites, and a random number. The random number matters later — it makes every session key unique even for identical traffic.

    2. Server hello and certificate

      The server picks one cipher suite, sends its own random number, and presents its X.509 certificate containing its public key and the signature of a certificate authority.

    3. The browser validates the certificate

      It checks that the CA's signature verifies against a root certificate already in its trust store, that the certificate has not expired or been revoked, and that the name on it matches the site being visited. This step is the entire basis of trust — a padlock means this check passed, nothing more.

    4. Key agreement

      Both sides run an ephemeral Diffie-Hellman exchange, arriving at a shared secret that was never transmitted. Combined with the two random numbers, it produces the symmetric session keys.

    5. Finished, then encrypted data

      Each side sends a "finished" message encrypted with the new keys, which simultaneously proves it derived them correctly and that no one tampered with the earlier, unencrypted handshake messages. All application data from here is symmetrically encrypted.

    Why both kinds of cryptography. Asymmetric cryptography solves a problem symmetric cannot — agreeing on a secret with someone you have never met, over a channel anyone can read. But it is roughly a thousand times slower. So it is used once, for the few hundred bytes of key agreement and identity proof, and then abandoned in favour of AES for the megabytes of actual traffic. Neither would do the job alone.

    Using ephemeral Diffie-Hellman gives forward secrecy: the session key is discarded afterwards and never derivable from the server's long-term private key. An attacker who records traffic today and steals the server key next year still cannot decrypt it. For a bank, that property is worth stating explicitly in an answer.

    (a) Where 64 kbps comes from

    1. Choose the sampling rate

      Nyquist's theorem says a signal must be sampled at at least twice its highest frequency, otherwise different input frequencies become indistinguishable after sampling — aliasing.

      Formulafs ≥ 2 × f(max)
      Substitutefs ≥ 2 × 4000 Hz
      Rate8000 samples per second
    2. Multiply by the bits per sample
      Formulabit rate = sampling rate × bits per sample
      Substitute8000 × 8
      Bit rate64,000 bps = 64 kbps — one DS0 channel

      Eight bits gives 2⁸ = 256 quantisation levels. This single number is the foundation of the whole digital telephone hierarchy: a T1 carrier is 24 of these channels, an E1 is 30.

    (c) Manchester's signal rate

    Manchester guarantees a transition in the middle of every bit, which is what lets the receiver recover the clock from the data alone. The cost is that each bit now contains two signal elements rather than one.

    Relationsignal rate = 2 × bit rate, for Manchester
    Substitute2 × 10 Mbps
    Signal rate20 Mbaud — so it needs roughly twice the bandwidth of NRZ
    That is the trade the whole of line coding turns on. NRZ is bandwidth-efficient but a long run of identical bits leaves the receiver with no transitions to lock onto, and its clock drifts. Manchester spends double the bandwidth to make loss of synchronisation impossible. Classic 10 Mbps Ethernet took that deal; faster Ethernet did not, and uses block codes such as 4B/5B instead — which guarantee enough transitions at only 25% overhead rather than 100%.

    (a) How distance vector works

    Each router knows only two things: the cost to its direct neighbours, and whatever those neighbours tell it. Every 30 seconds it sends its whole table to each neighbour, and on receiving a neighbour's table it applies one rule — if going via that neighbour is cheaper than what it currently has, adopt it. Nothing ever sees the whole topology. Routers converge by rumour, which works well until a link fails.

    The trouble starts on the third row. A has lost its route, but B is still advertising a cost of 1 to N — a route whose next hop is A itself. A has no way to know that, so it believes B and installs a route through B. The two now point at each other, and each update raises the cost by one until it hits RIP's ceiling of 16, which is defined as unreachable. Every packet for N loops between them for the minutes this takes.

    Split horizon Never advertise a route back out of the interface you learned it on. B would not have told A about N at all, so the loop never forms.
    Poison reverse Stronger version — advertise it back with a cost of infinity, so A is told explicitly that B is not a way to reach N.
    Hold-down timer After hearing a route has failed, ignore any new advertisement for it for a fixed period, letting the bad news propagate first.
    Triggered updates Send the change immediately instead of waiting for the 30-second timer, so bad news travels as fast as good news.

    Note that split horizon does not fix every case — a loop across three or more routers in a ring can still count up, which is ultimately why link-state protocols replaced distance vector on serious networks.

    (a) How 200 hosts fit behind one address

    Plain NAT maps one private address to one public address, so 200 hosts would need 200 public addresses and we have one.     (b) Is NAT security?

    It is a common exam trap, and the honest answer is no — but it does have a real side effect.

    The side effect: because the translation table is only populated by outbound connections, an unsolicited inbound packet has no matching entry and the NAT device has nowhere to send it. Internal hosts are therefore not directly reachable from the internet. That is genuine and useful.

    Why it is not a security control: it was designed to conserve addresses, not to enforce policy. It inspects nothing. It will happily carry malware out and its command-and-control traffic back, because that traffic rides on a connection the infected host opened itself. It gives no protection against phishing, a malicious download, a compromised internal host, or anything arriving over a permitted outbound session — which is how essentially all modern intrusions actually work. It also breaks end-to-end addressing, complicating VoIP, IPSec and peer-to-peer protocols.

    Treating NAT as a firewall is a documented cause of real breaches. IPv6 makes the point plainly: it has enough addresses that NAT is unnecessary, and no one argues that IPv6 networks are therefore less secure — because the firewall was always doing the security work.

    A DMZ exists because some servers must be reachable from the internet — the public website, the mail gateway, the internet banking front end — and those are precisely the machines most likely to be compromised.     (a) Deriving the T1 rate

    1. Find the frame rate from one channel

      Each channel is a 64 kbps PCM voice stream contributing 8 bits per frame. So the number of frames per second is fixed by how often each channel must be sampled.

      Formulaframe rate = channel bit rate / bits per slot
      Substitute64,000 bps / 8 bits
      Frame rate8000 frames per second — the PCM sampling rate, as expected
    2. Find the frame size
      Data bits24 channels × 8 bits = 192 bits
      Framing+ 1 bit, so the receiver can find the slot boundaries
      Frame193 bits
    3. Multiply out
      Line rate193 bits × 8000 frames/s
      T11,544,000 bps = 1.544 Mbps
      Frame time1 / 8000 = 125 µs
      Efficiency192 / 193 = 0.9948
      Gives99.48% — only the single framing bit is overhead

    (b) Why statistical TDM needs addresses

    In synchronous TDM the slot position is the address. Channel 7 always occupies the seventh slot of every frame, so the receiver identifies it by counting. That is why the framing bit matters so much — lose slot alignment and every channel is decoded as its neighbour.

    The weakness is that channel 7's slot is transmitted whether channel 7 has anything to say or not. On a link where sources are bursty, most slots carry padding.

    Statistical TDM fixes that by allocating slots on demand — a source gets a slot only when it has data. But now the receiver can no longer infer the source from the position, because the position varies from frame to frame. Each slot must therefore carry an explicit address.

    Tradesynchronous: no address bits, but wasted empty slots
    Againststatistical: address overhead per slot, but no waste — and it wins whenever sources are idle much of the time

    This is the same argument that separates circuit switching from packet switching one layer up, and packet headers are the same cost paid for the same benefit.

    ConsiderationCircuit switchingPacket switching

    Packet switching, without much hesitation. An ATM session is a handful of small messages lasting seconds, separated by long idle periods — the worst possible fit for a reserved circuit, which would sit unused most of the day while being billed for continuously. Packet switching also reroutes automatically around a failed link, which matters more for a dispersed ATM estate than the constant latency a circuit would give. In practice this is delivered as MPLS over the provider's network, which is packet-switched but adds the traffic engineering and quality-of-service guarantees that a bank needs, with IPSec VPN over broadband as a cheaper backup path. The one thing that must not be compromised is encryption: the transaction carries a PIN, so the link is protected end to end regardless of which switching technology carries it.
